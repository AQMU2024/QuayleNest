Complex Potentials

The Motivation

In the first semester of third year (2025), I took a class in fluid mechanics taught by Dr. Paul Watts. Overall, it was one of the most engaging modules I've ever taken, but in particular I became interested in modelling special types of fluid flow in two dimensions using complex potentials.

By taking an analytical shortcut through complex analysis, much of the computationally taxing simulations often associated with fluid mechanics can be foregone in exchange for a hit to the realism of the fluids and a loss of one measly dimension (which is good for my poor little computer.)

After my exams that semester, I set off to find out how far I could push these complex potentials.

Some Theory

If we take a fluid and impose the restriction that it must be incompressible, then the mass continuity equation reduces to ∇ ⋅ u → = 0 → . As the divergence of the curl of any vector field is equal to zero, we can define a vector potential ψ → such that u → = ∇ × ψ → . If we then impose the further restriction that the fluid be irrotational, then the vorticity field must be zero everywhere: ω → = ∇ × u → = 0 → . Using similar logic as before, we can note that the curl of any gradient is always zero, and so a scalar potential φ can be defined such that ∇ φ = u → .

Note that there is some freedom when it comes to choosing a gauge potential here, but for now we will skirt around the conversation regarding gauge fixing and let all gauge potentials equal zero.

In the quest to make these fluids as unrealistic as possible, we're going to make one more restriction. Restrict the fluid to only be defined in two dimensions, spanned by the standard orthonormal basis in ℝ 2 . We now have everything we need to start making some really cool connections.
